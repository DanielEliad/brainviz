# Application of the cross wavelet transform and wavelet coherence to geophysical time series (A. Grinsted, J. C. Moore & S. Jevrejeva, 2004)

*Nonlinear Processes in Geophysics (2004) 11: 561–566.*

## Overview

The paper addresses the lack of accessible tools for analyzing **two time series together** in time-frequency space. While the continuous wavelet transform (CWT) was already a common tool for finding localized, intermittent oscillations in a single series, the authors extend it to pairs of series via the **cross wavelet transform (XWT)** and **wavelet coherence (WTC)**. The contribution is both methodological (clarifying how to quantify the phase relationship and how to compute WTC significance) and practical (a freely available MatLab package). Their key argument is that regions of time-frequency space where two series share a **consistent phase relationship** are suggestive of a causal/mechanistic link. They demonstrate the methods on the Arctic Oscillation (AO) index and Baltic maximum sea ice extent (BMI), finding a robust anti-phase relationship across scales. Important caveats: the methods should be applied with "solid mechanistic foundations" rather than in a "scatter-gun" approach, since some datasets show significant links purely by chance; significance is assessed against red-noise (AR1) backgrounds using Monte Carlo methods.

## Method

**Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT).** A wavelet is "a function with zero mean and that is localized in both frequency and time." The CWT applies the wavelet as a **band-pass filter** to the series: the wavelet is stretched by varying its **scale** `s` and slid along time, and the transform is the convolution of the series with the scaled, normalized wavelet. Each point yields a complex number whose magnitude squared is the **wavelet power** `|W_n^X(s)|²` and whose complex argument is the **local phase**. Because the wavelet is not perfectly localized in time, edge artifacts arise near the start/end of the series; the **Cone of Influence (COI)** marks the region "in which edge effects can not be ignored" (defined where edge-induced power has dropped to e⁻² of its edge value). Results inside the COI must be interpreted with care.

**Morlet wavelet, scale vs. Fourier period.** The **Morlet wavelet** is a complex exponential modulated by a Gaussian, `ψ₀(η) = π^(-1/4) e^(iω₀η) e^(-η²/2)`. With dimensionless frequency `ω₀ = 6` it gives "a good balance between time and frequency localization," which is why it is preferred for feature extraction. A practical convenience: for ω₀=6 the **Fourier period is almost equal to the scale** (`λ_wt = 1.03 s`), so wavelet scale can be read directly as an oscillation period.

**Cross Wavelet Transform (XWT).** Given two series x and y with transforms W^X and W^Y, the XWT is `W^XY = W^X W^Y*` (* = complex conjugation). Its magnitude `|W^XY|` is the **cross wavelet power**, revealing where the two series share high common power. Crucially, the complex argument `arg(W_xy)` "can be interpreted as the local relative phase between x_n and y_n in time frequency space."

**Reading lead/lag from the phase angle.** The relative phase is displayed as **arrows** on the time-frequency plot. Per Fig. 3's convention: in-phase points right, anti-phase points left, and (in their example) "BMI leading AO by 90° pointing straight down." So the arrow direction encodes both whether the series move together/oppositely and **which one leads** — a phase that is not 0° or 180° indicates a lead/lag, and the sign of the offset tells you the direction of leadership. A **consistent or slowly-varying phase lag** across a region argues for a real physical link; a **constant phase angle across all scales** "argues for a constant time lag due to the physical mechanism of signal propagation." The mean phase over significant regions is computed with the **circular mean** of the angles (Eq. 6), with a **circular standard deviation** (Eq. 7) summarizing scatter. In their example the mean phase was −176±12°, i.e. essentially anti-phase, with a slight deviation hinting that AO leads BMI.

**Wavelet Coherence (WTC).** WTC is defined (following Torrence & Webster) as the cross wavelet power **smoothed** in both scale and time, normalized by the smoothed individual powers — `R_n²(s) = |S(s⁻¹ W^XY)|² / (S(s⁻¹|W^X|²) · S(s⁻¹|W^Y|²))`. The smoothing operator `S = S_scale(S_time(...))` smooths along both the scale axis and the time axis, with a footprint matched to the Morlet wavelet (a Gaussian in time, a rectangle of width 0.6·s in scale — 0.6 being the empirical scale decorrelation length). The result is "a localized correlation coefficient in time frequency space," ranging 0–1. WTC's advantage over XWT is that it "can find significant coherence even though the common power is low" — it uncovers **locally phase-locked behavior** — at the price of being slightly less localized in time-frequency.

**Significance testing.** Wavelet power significance is assessed against a null hypothesis of a stationary process with a **red-noise (AR1)** background spectrum (Eq. 3), whose lag-1 autocorrelation α is estimated from the data; the theoretical power distribution (Eq. 4) gives analytic confidence levels. For the XWT, the cross-power significance uses the product of two chi-squared distributions (Eq. 5; the 5% level uses Z₂(95%)=3.999). **WTC significance has no analytic form and must be found by Monte Carlo**: generate a large ensemble of surrogate series pairs with the same AR1 coefficients, compute WTC for each, and estimate the per-scale significance level from values outside the COI. They note the AR1 coefficient has little impact on the WTC significance level, but the **smoothing parameters have a large impact**; ~1000 surrogate pairs and 10 scales per octave are recommended.

## Key facts & quotes

- *"A wavelet is a function with zero mean and that is localized in both frequency and time."* — Defines the basic object; grounds why wavelets capture intermittent, time-localized oscillations that Fourier analysis cannot.
- *"The complex argument arg(W_xy) can be interpreted as the local relative phase between x_n and y_n in time frequency space."* — The mathematical basis for extracting **lead/lag/phase** from the XWT; central to any directional connectivity measure.
- *"The relative phase relationship is shown as arrows (with in-phase pointing right, anti-phase pointing left, and BMI leading AO by 90° pointing straight down)."* — The exact convention for reading leadership direction from phase, directly transferable to a visualization.
- *"Notice that this definition closely resembles that of a traditional correlation coefficient, and it is useful to think of the wavelet coherence as a localized correlation coefficient in time frequency space."* — Establishes WTC as a 0–1 local correlation; the intuition for "coherence" as connectivity strength.
- *"Where XWT unveils high common power, WTC finds locally phase locked behavior. The more desirable features of the WTC come at the price of being slightly less localized in time frequency space."* — Explains the practical tradeoff between XWT and WTC.
- *"The significance level of the WTC has to be determined using Monte Carlo methods."* — Why surrogate-based (AR1) significance testing is mandatory for coherence, not just analytic thresholds.
- *"The observation that the phase angles are constant across all scales argues for a constant time lag due to the physical mechanism of signal propagation."* — Justifies interpreting consistent phase as evidence of a real directional mechanism, not coincidence.

## Relevance to our tool

This paper is the methodological foundation for a **"wavelet lead coherence"** measure of directional connectivity between brain networks:

- **Directionality comes from the XWT phase angle.** Our pipeline classifies the cross-wavelet phase `angle(Wxy)` into lead / lag / in-phase / anti-phase bins (the ±45°/±135° quadrants), which is exactly the phase-arrow scheme of Fig. 3 here. A network "leading" another corresponds to a phase angle in the leading quadrant — the geophysical "BMI leads AO by 90°, arrow points down" is the same idea applied to two brain RSNs.
- **Coherence gives a strength/confidence gate.** WTC (the smoothed, normalized cross-power, range 0–1) acts as a localized correlation that tells us *where in time-scale space* the lead/lag relationship is trustworthy. Our masking of phase by `Rsq < 0.5` (and significance) mirrors the paper's practice of computing mean phase only over significant regions outside the COI.
- **A "proportion of lead points" measure is well-grounded.** Because phase is defined at every (time, scale) point, counting the fraction of points where network A leads network B — within a sliding window or scale band — is a natural aggregation of the per-point phase classification. The paper's use of the **circular mean** and **circular standard deviation** over significant regions provides the principled summary statistic and an uncertainty estimate for such an aggregation.
- **Honest visualization must respect the COI and significance.** Edge effects (COI) and red-noise significance are first-class concerns here; a faithful tool should exclude or de-emphasize COI regions and insignificant coherence, exactly as our angle maps set such points to "no coherence."
- **Scale ≈ period (Morlet ω₀=6).** Because λ_wt ≈ 1.03·s, scale axes can be labeled directly as oscillation periods (seconds, given our TR=2s), making time-frequency lead/lag plots interpretable to a brain-science audience.

### Plain-language essence (for an abstract sentence)

The wavelet transform breaks a single time series into a map of *which rhythms are present at which moments*, rather than assuming they stay fixed over the whole recording. Applied to a **pair** of signals, wavelet coherence measures — separately for each rhythm and each moment — how strongly the two move together (a local, time-and-frequency-resolved correlation between 0 and 1), while the cross-wavelet **phase** reveals whether they are synchronized and, critically, **which signal leads the other**. Together they turn two raw time series into a localized picture of when, at what timescale, how strongly, and in which direction one is coupled to the other.
