**Abstract**
Resting-state functional connectivity in autism is increasingly understood to be dynamic, with the coupling be-
tween brain networks rising and falling over a scan, yet the tools most researchers reach for collapse a recording
into a single static connectome and discard this temporal structure. We present an interactive web application for
exploring time-resolved functional connectivity in the large ABIDE I+II autism cohort. The system decomposes
each subject into 14 ICA-derived resting-state networks and computes connectivity between every network pair
within a sliding time window, using symmetric correlation (Pearson, Spearman) or an asymmetric wavelet mea-
sure that exposes directionality, then animates the resulting graph so that connection strength and lead or follow
direction can be scrubbed through time. A complementary overview lays out every subject’s connectivity matrix
by diagnosis (ASD vs. healthy control) and site and compares it against the class mean and median, supporting
visual identification of individual, group, and site differences. The result turns a large, otherwise opaque dataset
into something a domain expert can interrogate directly.

**2. Data and Connectivity**
Group ICA with dual regression yields, per subject, 32 component time series, of which 14 are kept as RSNs (default
mode, salience, visual, sensorimotor, auditory, frontoparietal, dorsal attention and language networks) [SPA ∗ 25], giving a T × 14 activity matrix. A window of fixed length slides
across it, and at each step a 14 × 14 connectivity matrix is
computed over all network pairs, producing a sequence of
frames.
The system offers three measures. Pearson and Spearman
correlation give symmetric matrices in [ − 1 , 1]. The third
is directional. Wavelet coherence acts as a localized cor
relation coefficient in time-frequency space, and its crosswavelet phase angle gives the lead or lag between two signals [GMJ04, TC98], classifying each network pair as inphase, anti-phase, leading, or lagging [BAZ17,CPB ∗ 23]. Per
window, it reports a phase-leading ratio in 0 , 1, the fraction of time-frequency points at which one network leads
the other, computed in both directions, so the wavelet matrix is asymmetric ( A → B ̸ = B → A ) and encodes which network drives which, a directional reading absent from correlation [CPB ∗ 23].
**3. Visualization**
The graph view places the 14 networks on a ring and draws
the current frame’s connectivity as edges (Figure 2). Color
encodes the coupling’s sign on a blue to red diverging scale
and thickness its magnitude, and a threshold hides weak
edges. Directional wavelet edges are curved and carry arrowheads that show which network leads. A timeline scrubs or
plays the frames, animating how the graph reconfigures, and
hovering a network isolates its edges. The view also plays
aggregate sequences, the mean or median over all ASD or

**4. Comparative Overview**
A complementary overview (Figure 1) reduces each subject to a single matrix over the whole recording, shown as a
heatmap or graph glyph in healthy control and ASD columns
grouped by site. Read against the class mean and median, it
supports rapid scanning for individual outliers, group contrasts, and site variation, a known confound in multisite rs
fMRI.
